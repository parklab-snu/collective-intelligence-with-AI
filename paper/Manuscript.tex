\section*{Abstract}

Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have enabled the wide use of personalized assistance for cognitive tasks, which enhanced individual performance on various fields.
However, the impact of AI assistance on collective intelligence remains poorly understood. AI assistance may undermine the emergence of collective intelligence by reducing the individual diversity or introducing structured biases into a population. 
Here, we develop a human-AI collective prediction model mediated by social learning based on incentive structure by extending the existing works on collective intelligence.
Our model uncovers that emergence of collective intelligence is depend on how human and AI interact. 
When AI works as if it is omniscient and gives assistance regardless of users' interests, collective intelligence will be promoted.
However, when AI works as a chat-bot and gives assistance only based on users' interests, collective intelligence could be hindered due to diversity loss and overconfidence on AI.
We showed that, to promote collective intelligence, penalizing or incentivizing AI usage is not reliable. Instead, elaborating more diversity-aware incentives could be the key to promote and maintain the collective intelligence.

\section*{Introduction}

Collective intelligence (CI) describes a phenomenon in which a group’s ability to solve problems, make decisions, or adapt to changing environments exceeds each member’s ability \cite{galton1907vox,woolley2010evidence,yaniv2007using,hommes2005coordination,lorge1958survey}.
This phenomenon, also referred to as wisdom of crowds, has thus served as a central mechanism underlying the success of diverse organizations, ranging from animal groups \cite{dreyer2025comparing,simons2004many,berdahl2013emergent} and human societies \cite{woolley2010evidence, riedl2021quantifying} to machine-learning systems \cite{breiman1996bagging,breiman2001random}.
Understanding the conditions that promote and  maintain CI therefore remains a fundamental question across social sciences \cite{mann2017optimal}, evolutionary biology \cite{wang2025individual}, and computer science \cite{werfel2026fluid}.
Addressing this question has become particularly timely as artificial intelligence (AI) has begun to reshape how individuals acquire information, make decisions, and contribute to collective outcomes \cite{cui2024ai}.

The recent emergence of generative AIs and large language models (LLMs) has changed the way individuals engage in various cognitive tasks, ranging from simple everyday tasks to more complex tasks, including scientific discovery \cite{lu2026towards} and creative writing \cite{doshi2024generative}.
In particular, while the use of personal AI models, such as ChatGPT, has been rapidly increasing \cite{sajadieh2026artificial}, the potential long-term impact of personal AI services on human society, especially the CI, remains uncertain.
Some argue that AI can promote CI by enhancing individual expertise \cite{vaccaro2024combinations}, and opinion aggregation \cite{burton2024large}.
Conversely, empirical evidence indicate that the use of AIs can limit the diversity of opinions \cite{doshi2024generative,meincke2025chatgpt, fugener2021will}, a key requirement for the emergence of CI \cite{lorenz2011social,aplin2014individual,hong2004groups}.

Our ability to predict the long-term impact of personal AI services on human CI is hampered by the apparent discrepancy in the incentives that shape AI uses.
On one hand, many industrial sectors are pushing towards increased AI usages, where the number of ``tokens'' used is often perceived as a proxy for productivity \cite{bousquette2026tokenmaxxing,keary2026cult}.
On the other hand, many academic institutions appear to discourage, and even penalize, AI use, reflecting concerns about plagiarism, misinformation, and limited independnce \cite{chawla2026researchers,thorp2023chatgpt}.
Such discrepancy raises a question about whether directly incentivizing or penalizing AI usage will benefit or harm human CI.
These observations further highlight the importance of indentifying a robust incentive structure that can promote CI in the presence of AI, which can retain the benefits of AIs while preventing potential risk such as preserving the diversity of individual opinions.

To address these questions, we extend previous approaches for modeling CI, where each individual makes independent predictions for the outcome from partial observations of underlying causal factors \cite{mann2017optimal,wang2025individual,hong2012incentives}.
We allow each individual to independently consult with personal AIs and revise their predictions accordingly.
Across different assumptions about the AI model's ability and underlying incentive structures, we investigate the robustness of human CIs.

\section*{Main}
\subsection*{Model}

We begin by extending previously established models of CI in which a group of players participates in a prediction task, with each player making an individual prediction based on partial observations of the underlying causal factors and the group forming a collective prediction by aggregating these individual predictions \cite{hong2012incentives, mann2017optimal,wang2025individual}.
The prediction target $(Y)$ is modeled as a linear combination of $M$ causal factors ($x_i$), with fixed coefficients ($\alpha_i$): $Y = \sum_{i = 1}^{M} \alpha_i x_i + \alpha_0$, where each factor is drawn from independent normal distribution.
Among $M$ different factors, an individual ($A$) makes prediction by the multiplication of single factor of interest $(x_{e_A})$ and their own belief $(c_A)$: $Y_{A} = c_A x_{e_A}$.
Here, we assume each individual can independently consult AI and revise their prediction based on their reliance on AI ($\beta_A$): $Y_A^{re} = \beta_A Y_A^{AI} + (1 - \beta_A) Y_A$, and the revised prediction is aggregated to form collective prediction.

To model AI, we consider a scenario where the group of players is given a novel prediction task, whereas the AI has an ability to perform prediction based on causal factors because it already been trained on similar tasks.
Every time a player consults AI, it will make predictions $(Y_A^{AI})$  based on its internal parameters, which can include factor-specific bias $(b_i)$ and stochastic error $(\epsilon)$.
We then consider two extreme types of AI, which differ in the amount of information AI uses when making predictions.
At one extreme, the AI operates with complete knowledge of all causal factors, using them regardless of the user’s interest:
$Y_{A}^{AI} = \sum_{i = 0}^{M}(\alpha_{i}x_{i} + b_{i}) + \epsilon$.
At the other extreme, AI can work like a chatbot, in which AI predicts the outcome based only on the user's interest:
$Y_{A}^{AI} = \alpha_{e_A}x_{e_A} + b_{e_{A}} + \epsilon$.
Hereafter, we refer to these two types as `Omniscient AI' and `Chatbot AI', respectively.

Each generation consists of multiple round of predictions, and each player is rewarded by a payoff according to the incentive structure.
For simplicity, we assume that a sufficiently large number of prediction rounds occurs within each generation, allowing us to use the expected payoff rather than computing the payoff for each round.
We consider two incentive structures, ``feedback'' and ``niche-expert'' \cite{wang2025individual}, which have been shown to promote near-perfect collective accuracy in the absence of AI (except averaging aggregation with niche-expert structure).
The feedback structure gives higher payoff to individuals who can push the collective prediction toward the true target, regardless of their individual predictive accuracy.
In contrast, the niche-expert structure gives higher payoff to individuals who have both better predictive accuracy and a underrepresented interest.

Through time, the group evolves by imitating the peers who receive higher payoffs.
Specifically, after each generation, two individuals are randomly sampled, and one imitates all three strategies (factor of interest, belief, reliance on AI) of the other with a probability determined by the difference between their expected payoffs.
Individual predictions are aggregated into a collective prediction ($\hat{Y}$) by simple averaging when using Omniscient AI and by clustering when using Chatbot AI.
Collective accuracy is defined as the reduction in prediction error achieved by the collective prediction relative to a baseline prediction ($\alpha_0$).
Hereafter, we refer to the near-perfect collective accuracy as accurate CI.

This model now allows us to explore the impact of AI on CI depending on the mode of AI and the incentive structure.
Here, we begin by comparing the emergence of CI with and without AI and subsequently investigate a better incentive structure that can promote CI in the presence of AI.

\subsection*{AI can either promote or prevent the emergence of CI}
To understand the impact of AI, we first compare collective dynamics with and without AI.
We find that, except when Omniscient AI is used under the feedback structure, a group with AI fails to achieve accurate CI (Fig. 2).
However, the effect of AI differs depending on the type of AI.

When Omniscient AI assists players, the group becomes equally or more accurate than the group without AI.
Under the feedback structure, a group achieves accurate CI regardless of the assistance of AI (Fig. 2A).
However, under the niche-expert structure, the group without AI is no better than the baseline prediction.
Although Omniscient AI cannot help players achieve accurate CI, the group's prediction with AI is more accurate than both that of the group without AI and the AI itself (Fig. 2B).
These results are robust across different levels of AI accuracy and can be shown mathematically by assuming a sufficiently large group of players (Fig. S2).

When Chatbot AI assists players, the group becomes less accurate than the group without AI.
Although both incentive structures can achieve accurate CI without AI, the assistance of Chatbot AI hinders group performance and prevents accurate CI from emerging (Fig. 2C, D).
This occurs through different mechanisms depending on the incentive structure.
Under the feedback structure, Chatbot AI leads to a loss of interest diversity, which eventually makes it impossible for the group to account for variation in some causal factors.
This leads to an increase in collective variance and stochastic decrease in collective accuracy (Fig. S1).
The loss of interest diversity may arise even without AI after sufficiently many generations in a finite population.
However, Chatbot AI makes the group more vulnerable to the diversity loss.
Under the niche-expert structure, the group becomes overly reliant on AI, causing the collective prediction to inherit the AI's bias (Fig. S1).
This leads to an increase in collective bias and a decrease in collective accuracy.
The niche-expert structure may also drive reliance on AI toward zero, depending on the relationship between the true coefficient and AI bias (Fig. S2).
In this case, the group completely excludes AI assistance and builds CI on its own, which substantially delays the emergence of accurate CI.

To investigate whether the group can retain its predictive ability when AI assistance suddenly becomes unavailable at a given generation, we measure counterfactual collective accuracy.
This measure follows the same evolutionary trajectory as the simulation with AI, but accuracy is computed using the players' unrevised predictions.
We find that counterfactual collective accuracy is always lower than the collective accuracy of groups, regardless of the AI mode or incentive structure.

In summary, AI assistance may promote CI when it is omniscient, whereas AI operating with limited information may prevent the emergence of accurate CI.
These findings underscore the need for incentive structures that can mitigate the potential risks associated with AI assistance.


\subsection*{Directly incentivizing or penalizing AI use can additionally harm CI}

Given the strong influence of AI on collective outcomes, a straightforward intervention is to directly incentivize or penalize the use of AI.
To investigate whether such direct incentives or penalties help emergence of CI, we modify the two incentive structures by adding a payoff term proportional to individual reliance on AI:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Feedback /} \mathrm{Niche\text{-}expert} + \lambda \beta_A
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ represent the strength of incentive ($\lambda > 0$) or penalty ($\lambda < 0$).

Directly incentivizing or penalizing AI use further reduce the collective accuracy (Fig. 3C, E, G), except when Omniscient AI is used under the feedback structure (Fig. 3A).
This is because incentivizing or penalizing AI usage pushes the group to always ($\beta = 1$) or never ($\beta = 0$) rely on AI, respectively (Fig. 3B, D, F, H).
In some cases, when AI bias is relatively large and the penalty is weak, penalizing AI use can achieve higher collective accuracy than the original incentive structure.
However, in most cases, penalizing AI use imposes strong selection against AI reliance, pushing the group toward lower stationary collective accuracy.
Although the group with Chatbot AI under feedback structure shows stochastic breakdown of CI which doesn't result in stationary collective accuracy, incentivizing or penalizing AI use further strengthens the breakdown (Fig. 3E).
This result suggests that simply incentivizing or penalizing the AI use may exacerbate rather than resolving the problem of AI.

\subsection*{Balanced incentives mitigate AI-induced failures of CI}
Neither the original incentive structures nor simple interventions targeting AI use are sufficient to promote CI.
What incentive structure, then, would work in the presence of AI?
Focusing on the Chatbot AI, in which proposed incentive structures fail to promote accurate CI, we investigate whether a better structure can overcome the risks introduced by AI.
Here, we propose a ``balanced'' structure, defined as the simple average of the feedback and niche expert incentives.

Core idea behind the balanced structure is to maintain interest diversity while preventing the collective bias of the group.
The feedback structure is prone to interest diversity loss, whereas can strongly prevent collective bias by incentivizing players whose predictions lie opposite to the collective prediction.
By contrast, the niche-expert structure can maintain interest diversity because it directly incentivizes minority interests.
However it cannot prevent collective bias because, within the same factor of interest, individuals are rewarded only according to their individual predictive performance, which homogenize the predictions. 

By integrating both structure, the balanced structure can promote accurate CI by maintaining both interest diversity and preventing collective bias (Fig. 4, S3).
Compare to the feedback and niche-expert, players are both evenly distributed through factors, and beliefs are close to the real coefficient (Fig. 4).
Moreover, counterfactual collective accuracy maintained high compare to both feedback and niche-expert.
These patterns are driven by the emergent property of the balanced structure, in which group increases its reliance on AI as the AI becomes more accurate, demonstrating appropriately calibrated AI use.


\section*{Discussion}

In this paper, we study how AI and incentive structures affect the emergence of CI. 
Our model suggests that the effect of AI depends on the type of AI used by the group.
AI that can incorporate all relevant causal factors may improve group performance, whereas AI that relies on users to gather information about the causal factors may impair it.
By examining different variants of incentive structures, we further show that direct interventions targeting AI use generally harm group performance.
Instead, we suggest balanced structure which can promote CI while also generating appropriately calibrated AI use as an emergent property.

Although Omniscient AI can produce group performance that is equal to or better than that achieved without AI, such AI may be difficult to realize in practice because it needs to be able to observe causal factors independently.
Achieving this capability would require hardwares, such as sensors, as well as autonomous data acquisition capabilities.
For this reason, most AI services currently available are better represented by the Chatbot AI in our model, which highlights the potential risks of AI use for CI.

In our model, individuals revise their predictions based on the AI’s assistance.
It is therefore unsurprising that AI assistance reduces the diversity of individual predictions, a pattern that has also been observed empirically.
However, a reduction in prediction diversity does not necessarily lead to the collapse of CI.
The principal risk arises when the incentive structure fails to guide the group toward an appropriate set of strategies.
The feedback structure leads to the breakdown of CI because it recruits more players to more important factors, which have larger coefficients. 
This characteristic creates a risk of interest-diversity loss in finite populations, a situation in which no player remains interested in certain factors because most players shift toward relatively important factors.
Without AI, this occurs when initial individual predictions are not sufficiently diverse or when there is no source of novel ideas, such as mutation.
Revising individual predictions with AI assistance exacerbates this problem by pulling individuals’ predictions toward the AI’s prediction, thereby reducing prediction diversity even under the same belief distribution.
The niche-expert structure hinders CI because it favors extreme levels of reliance on AI.
Because individuals within each interest group are affected only by their own performance, incentives based on individual performance reach their maximum at either full reliance on AI or no reliance at all.
This extreme reliance results in suboptimal collective accuracy or substantially delays the emergence of CI.

Because AI already generates substantial benefits as well as costs, organizations increasingly seek to improve collective performance by regulating AI use.
Such interventions may take direct forms, including AI-use policies or token-based leaderboards, or may operate indirectly through other incentives, such as productivity rewards or the social stigma associated with AI use.
However, our model suggests that directly incentivizing or penalizing AI use is unlikely to induce appropriately calibrated reliance on AI or to promote CI.
Incentivizing AI use could lead to overreliance, causing groups to propagate AI biases, whereas penalizing AI use could lead to underreliance, preventing groups from using AI even when it performs well in the relevant domain.
Our result instead shows that appropriate AI use and accurate CI can be promoted by focusing on the classic properties of groups that have been emphasized for decades—maintaining diversity and preventing collective bias—rather than on AI use itself.


The consistently limited counterfactual collective accuracy observed across AI types and incentive structures suggests that the presence of AI can degrade human group’s own performance, consistent with empirical evidence.
This may reduce collective performance when AI assistance becomes unavailable because of resource constraints, system failure, or model collapse, forcing the group to rebuild CI through further adaptation.
The higher counterfactual collective accuracy achieved under the balanced structure indicates that an appropriate incentive structure can mitigate the risk of CI breakdown following the loss of AI assistance. 

Our model and analysis have several limitations. 
First, the model considers only social learning among humans and treats the influence of AI as unidirectional.
In reality, humans can learn from AI, while AI systems can also be further trained on human-generated data.
Such bidirectional adaptation may further reduce group performance by increasing the similarity between human and AI predictions and thereby weakening their complementarity. Empirical studies of human–AI collaboration likewise suggest that stronger correlations between human and AI predictions can diminish the benefits of collaboration.
Second, we model AI errors using factor-specific additive biases and random error.
Previous studies of AI bias and error suggest that additive biases may arise from skewed training data, or systematic calibration.
However, AI errors may also exhibit more complex structures, including biased estimates of factor effects and correlations across prediction errors.
Further empirical research is therefore needed to characterize the structure of AI bias and error, together with modeling approaches that can incorporate these more complex patterns.
Finally, to retain the tractability of established CI models, we assume that the prediction target is a linear combination of causal factors, which does not hold for many real-world tasks.
In particular, many machine learning methods used in AI systems are capable of capturing nonlinear relationships. Future research should therefore investigate how nonlinear relationships between causal factors and prediction targets affect the interaction between AI assistance and the emergence of CI.     


\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Prediction target}

Following previous models of CI, we model the prediction target as a linear combination of $M$ independent causal factors:

\begin{equation}
Y = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 X_1 + \cdots + \alpha_M X_M,
\end{equation}

where the fixed coefficient $\alpha_i$ determines the magnitude and direction of the effect of factor $i$, and $\alpha_0$ is a global intercept. Each causal factor $X_i$ is modeled as an independent Gaussian random variable with mean zero and standard deviation $\sigma_i$.


\subsection*{Players}

We consider a well-mixed population $N$ consisting of $n$ players engaged in a novel prediction task. In the absence of AI assistance, the strategy of player $A$ is characterized by two variables: interest $e_A$ and belief $c_A$. Player $A$ observes the causal factor $X_{e_A}$ and predicts the target by multiplying the observed factor value by their belief:

\begin{equation}
Y_A = c_A X_{e_A},
\end{equation}

where $e_A$ can take an integer value from 0 to $M$. When $e_A=0$, the player does not observe any causal factor and instead makes a constant prediction based solely on their belief. We refer to this as a stubborn strategy because the player's prediction does not respond to the realized values of the causal factors. For notational convenience, we assume that $X_0=1$.

When the group is assisted by AI, each player's strategy additionally includes reliance on AI, denoted by $\beta_A$. We assume that each player consults the AI independently and revises their prediction by taking a weighted average of their own prediction and the AI-generated prediction:

\begin{equation}
Y_A^{\mathrm{re}} = \beta_A Y_A^{\mathrm{AI}} + (1-\beta_A)Y_A,
\end{equation}

where $\beta_A$ ranges from 0 to 1. A value of $\beta_A=1$ indicates complete reliance on AI, whereas $\beta_A=0$ indicates that the player does not use the AI prediction.


\subsection*{AI models}
We assume that the AI model has been trained on similar tasks and is therefore capable of making predictions based on causal factors. However, because the model is imperfect, its outputs are subject to systematic additive bias and stochastic error. The additive bias associated with factor $i$ is denoted by $b_i$, and the stochastic error is denoted by $\epsilon$. The AI is neither trained nor updated during the simulation, and its biases therefore remain fixed throughout the simulation. Although the bias is indexed by factor, all $b_i$, including $b_0$, are assigned the same value in the main analyses for simplicity. The model formulation nevertheless allows biases to differ among factors.

We assume that the stochastic error follows a Gaussian distribution with mean zero and standard deviation $\tau$. Each time a player consults the AI, an independent error is generated. Errors are therefore independent across players and across repeated consultations by the same player. Consequently, stochastic AI errors can be attenuated when independently generated predictions are aggregated across players. We therefore held $\tau$ constant and varied the magnitude of the additive bias to manipulate the level of AI accuracy across analyses.

We consider two extreme types of AI that differ in the amount of information available for prediction. The first is termed the Omniscient AI, which can observe all $M$ causal factors and incorporate them into its prediction regardless of the user's interest. The prediction generated by the Omniscient AI for player $A$ is expressed as follows:

\begin{equation}
Y_A^{\mathrm{AI}} = \alpha_0 + b_0 + \alpha_1 X_1 + b_1 + \cdots + \alpha_M X_M + b_M + \epsilon.
\end{equation}

The second is termed the Chatbot AI, which can use only the single causal factor $X_{e_A}$ associated with the player's interest. The prediction generated by the Chatbot AI for player $A$ is expressed as follows:

\begin{equation}
Y_A^{\mathrm{AI}} = \alpha_{e_A} X_{e_A} + b_{e_A} + \epsilon.
\end{equation}


\subsection*{Aggregation rules}

The revised individual predictions are aggregated to form a collective prediction. We use two aggregation rules proposed in previous research, depending on the type of AI used by the group.

When the group is assisted by the Omniscient AI, the AI component of each revised prediction contains information about all $M$ factors, even though the individual's own prediction is based on only one factor. Each revised prediction can therefore be treated as an estimate of the entire target. Accordingly, we calculate the collective prediction by taking the simple average of the revised individual predictions:

\begin{equation}
\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{re}} = \frac{Y_1^{\mathrm{re}} + Y_2^{\mathrm{re}} + \cdots + Y_n^{\mathrm{re}}}{n}.
\end{equation}

When the group is assisted by the Chatbot AI, both the player's initial prediction and the AI's prediction are based on the same causal factor. Each revised prediction therefore contains information about only one factor and can be treated as an estimate of that factor's contribution to the target. We consequently use clustering aggregation.

Clustering aggregation is performed by grouping individuals according to their interests and calculating the mean prediction within each group. The collective prediction is then obtained by summing the mean predictions of all nonempty groups:

\begin{equation}
\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{re}} = \sum_{i:n_i>0} \frac{1}{n_i} \sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}_i} Y_A^{\mathrm{re}},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{A}_i$ is the set of players interested in factor $i$, and $n_i=|\mathcal{A}_i|$ is the number of players in that set.

When the group is not assisted by AI, collective predictions are computed from the unrevised individual predictions throughout the simulation. Both simple averaging and clustering can be used in the absence of AI. For fair comparison, we use the same aggregation rule as in the corresponding AI-assisted condition.


\subsection*{Incentive structures}

Players receive payoffs determined by an incentive structure based on their strategies. We consider five incentive structures: feedback structure, niche-expert structure, direct intervention on AI use of these two structures, and a balanced structure.

The feedback structure does not directly reward individuals according to their own prediction accuracy. Instead, it rewards predictions that are aligned with the residual error of the collective prediction and therefore have the potential to move the collective prediction toward the true value. Given player $A$'s revised prediction $Y_A^{\mathrm{re}}$ and the collective prediction $\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{re}}$, the payoff is expressed as

\begin{equation}
\pi_A = Y_A^{\mathrm{re}}(Y-\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{re}}).
\end{equation}

The niche-expert structure assigns higher payoffs to individuals who make accurate predictions while having an underrepresented interest. Specifically, the squared prediction error of player $A$ is weighted by $\rho_{e_A}=n_{e_A}/n$, the proportion of players interested in the same factor:

\begin{equation}
\pi_A = -\rho_{e_A}(Y_A^{\mathrm{re}}-Y)^2.
\end{equation}

Previous research has shown that these two incentive structures can produce nearly perfect CI through social learning, except when the niche-expert incentive is combined with simple averaging. They are therefore used as baseline structures for examining how AI assistance affects the emergence of CI.

To examine the effects of directly encouraging or discouraging AI use, we modify the feedback and niche-expert structures by adding a term proportional to the player's reliance on AI:

\begin{equation}
\pi_A = Y_A^{\mathrm{re}}(Y-\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{re}}) + \lambda\beta_A.
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\pi_A = -\rho_{e_A}(Y_A^{\mathrm{re}}-Y)^2 + \lambda\beta_A.
\end{equation}

Here, $\lambda$ is a weighting coefficient representing the strength of the direct incentive or penalty applied to AI use. A positive value of $\lambda$ rewards AI reliance, whereas a negative value penalizes it. When $\lambda=0$, no direct incentive or penalty is applied, and the payoff functions reduce to the original feedback and niche-expert structures.

The balanced structure is defined as the arithmetic mean of the feedback and niche-expert payoff components:

\begin{equation}
\pi_A = \frac{Y_A^{\mathrm{re}}(Y-\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{re}}) - \rho_{e_A}(Y_A^{\mathrm{re}}-Y)^2}{2}.
\end{equation}


\subsection*{Simulations}

All simulations were conducted using an agent-based model with 50 causal factors ($M=50$) and 10,000 players ($n=10{,}000$). The parameters defining the prediction target were sampled at the beginning of the simulations and subsequently held fixed.

The coefficients $\alpha_i$were sampled from a uniform distribution on $[-5,5]$, except in the simulation presented in Figure~4, in which $\alpha_i$ was set to $5-0.2i$ for visualization. The standard deviations $\sigma_i$ were sampled from a uniform distribution on $[0,3]$.

Players' initial strategies were also sampled from predefined probability distributions. The distribution of individual belief $c_A$ depended on the aggregation rule. Under simple averaging, each factor's contribution is scaled by the proportion of players interested in that factor. We therefore sampled beliefs from a Gaussian distribution with mean zero and standard deviation 100, following the scaling used in previous work \cite{wang2025individual}. 
Under clustering aggregation, beliefs were sampled from a Gaussian distribution with mean zero and standard deviation 5. Because predictions are averaged separately within each interest group and then summed, no additional scaling of $c_A$ is required. 
The initial interest of each player was sampled uniformly from the integers between 0 and 50, and reliance on AI was sampled from a uniform distribution on $[0,1]$.

We assumed that prediction events occurred on a substantially faster timescale than social learning. At each generation, we therefore calculated the expected payoff $\mathbb{E}[\pi_A]$ of each player analytically rather than explicitly sampling prediction events. The expectation was taken over the distributions of the causal factors $X_1,\ldots,X_M$ and the stochastic AI error $\epsilon$, conditional on the current population strategies and the fixed target parameters. Closed form expression of expected payoffs were derived for all incentive structures and are presented in the Supplementary Methods.

Each generation consisted of an expected payoff calculation followed by one opportunity for pairwise social learning. Two distinct players, $A$ and $B$, were randomly sampled, and player $A$ imitated all three strategies of player $B$ with probability

\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(s\left(\mathbb{E}[\pi_A]-\mathbb{E}[\pi_B]\right)\right)},
\end{equation}

where $s$ represents the strength of social learning and was set to 50 in all simulations. We introduced a mutation rate $\mu$, such that with probability $\mu$, player $A$ did not imitate player $B$ but instead redrew all strategy components from the same distributions used to initialize the population. The mutation rate was set to zero in all simulations except those presented in Supplementary Figure~4.

Unless otherwise specified, each simulation was run for 200,000 generations. When a result was represented by a single summary value rather than as a temporal trajectory, the metric was averaged over the final 10,000 generations.

To evaluate the dynamics of collective prediction, we defined six summary metrics: collective accuracy, collective bias, collective variance, counterfactual collective accuracy, interest diversity, and median reliance on AI. At each population state, expectations and variances were evaluated analytically over the distributions of the causal factors and AI errors rather than estimated from sampled prediction events. Collective bias and collective variance constitute the bias--variance decomposition of the group level collective prediction error.

Collective accuracy was normalized relative to the baseline prediction $\alpha_0$. A negative value indicates that the collective prediction performs worse than the baseline. Counterfactual collective accuracy was evaluated by setting $\beta_A=0$ while retaining the evolved interests and beliefs of the AI-assisted population. In calculating interest diversity, we used the convention that $\rho_i\ln\rho_i=0$ when $\rho_i=0$ (Table~\ref{tab:evaluation_metrics}).
